# Designing by Abstention

*On knowing what AI should hold back, and why that is our job now. Adapted from a talk given at the School of Design, Hochschule Pforzheim, on 11 November 2026.*

Almost nine years ago, I set out to tackle what felt like the most challenging design project of my career.

The goal was radical: make building and testing an idea accessible to anyone, not only to those with a full product team behind them. We called it Senz.

We trained a first AI, a vision model that translated wireframes and whiteboard diagrams into working, testable applications, just from a simple photo taken with a phone.

Our reality check: one expert, drawing on a whiteboard, turned a new insurance concept into a working application inside Allianz's design system, with real data and pricing.

From that day on, my team and I spent four years turning that proof of concept into a reliable assistive design tool: a business logic engine, white-label design systems, and user research with AI-generated insight reports.

What we learned along the way was not what to add, but what our AI should abstain from. That's what let it empower humans, rather than replace them.

I have been a designer for more than twenty years, across three continents. But before everything, I'm a father. A maker. A thinker. A human, with the same concerns as yours. I'm writing this as all four.

## A tool, not a verdict

Those concerns often go by one name today: AI. Job disruption, disinformation, the flattening of critical thinking, the averaging of culture. These fears are real. But they come from one corner of AI, not the whole field. Whatever doomers or techno-optimists say, it's still a tool. An impressive one, but a tool.

A tool that promises to make the cost of production drop close to zero.

So if you measure your worth as a designer by the number of screens or renderings you hand off, you will feel threatened.

But if you treat AI not as a mere generator, but as a way to feed your curiosity, learn new skills, and explore new territories, it doesn't take your job. It becomes a compass, pointing to problems worth solving.

Let me go further. If AI makes production effortless and puts building within our reach, the human problems that were never profitable enough no longer wait for someone else's budget. By committing to solving them with AI, we learn how this technology works, and that knowledge gives us the authority to steer what it should become for our society. That's how our profession regains its influence, and with it, its responsibility.

## Holding something away, on purpose

And taking that responsibility often starts with knowing what to hold back. This is a method I'd practised for years without noticing, until I named it: designing by abstention. Borrowed from the Latin *abs*, away, and *tenere*, to hold: holding something away, on purpose.

In design, abstention is a decision, not a reflex. It is not a shortcut to the easiest answer. It is not a plain no, refusing out of fear or ignorance. Nor is it a plain yes, taking everything for granted.

Abstention is where we judge what could happen, and act on it, at every step. First, judging whether you need AI at all. Then, knowing its limits when you author with it. And most importantly, preventing its side effects when you build it into what you make. Abstention is the third path. "But" is the modulator. It works both ways: you can accept with a condition, or refuse with an alternative.

Abstention takes mastery: you cannot decide where AI should step back unless you know how it operates. When you master your craft, you can delegate, because you can judge what comes back. When you don't, you're offloading, and everything it returns becomes, if you're honest, a new doubt to resolve. Done with care, that checking is how you grow.

But knowing the technology isn't enough. You also need an ethical grounding, to know why it matters, and the critical thinking to evaluate where it builds human value, and where it strips human agency away. That's what abstention is: a position you earn, through experience and learning. One that lets you explain why you hold back, and stand by it.

## Three "buts"

### Accept, with a condition

What businesses fear losing is retention, satisfaction, adoption. Dowgo's CTO asked me to simplify their data room: buttons were everywhere. But the interface wasn't the problem. Most of those buttons existed only because files arrived unsorted.

The plain yes was easy: plug in an LLM and let it sort. But in due diligence, a document is there and right, or it isn't. There is no room for an answer someone invented. So, yes to AI: a small language-understanding model that reads file names, their context and their language. But it generates nothing, and what it doesn't recognise is never guessed: it goes to a person.

Founders drop their files, and the files land in the right folders. I didn't remove buttons. I removed the reasons for them.

### Refuse, with an alternative

What families fear is losing their data, and their kids hooked on screens. Vrooom is a hand-planed wooden car that helps children discover the playgrounds around them.

The plain yes is what most products do: an account, a profile, points and streaks to keep the child coming back. The plain no is a toy, and nothing else. Vrooom is a no, but. No account, but the car is the child's identity. No data stored online, but everything stays on the phone. No streaks, but badges you print, colour and glue into a passport.

There is no AI inside Vrooom. But it was authored with AI: it helped me break down the complexity of not signing a user in. To recognise the child, I didn't need to know who he is.

### When the stakes are highest

Some problems are too big to be framed. Maybe they aren't problems at all, just the rough path of life. You can't solve them. So you frame a problem by cutting a small window into what you believe could be solved.

My daughters live 13,000 km away. Through that window, I fell in love with one problem: how can I stay present in their days? Not in every moment. In the rituals we had together: in the morning, in the evening, and during the day.

Babiban answers it with perhaps the most dangerous part of generative AI: voice cloning. A device that talks with my voice, in Mandarin, a language I don't speak well. The plain no: voice cloning is dangerous, leave it alone. The plain yes: plug in a chatbot, and let a machine improvise as their father.

Babiban is a yes, but. Yes to the voice. No to the invention. It understands what they ask, then answers only with lines I wrote myself. It runs offline, so nothing they say leaves the device. And when they ask something I never wrote an answer for, it doesn't pretend.

Everything it says, I can stand behind.

## Where to start tomorrow

**Fall in love with a problem.** A real human problem, one you're happy to wake up for in the morning, or one that keeps you awake at night. And keep track of every decision you make along the way. This is where AI earns its place: you fall in love with the problem, it keeps the record. Every move, every choice, in plain text you can revisit one day.

**Doubt.** When you start doubting, you start creating. And this is where AI is useful: it can harvest hundreds of sources and condense them for you. Done right, it hands you every link, so you can check each one yourself. That's how you move from doubt to beyond reasonable doubt.

**To say "but", you need something to oppose.** So stay curious. Look for the ideas you disagree with, and the fields you know nothing about. Every idea is a thesis waiting for its antithesis. What you make of the two, the synthesis, is yours alone: it's your uniqueness. AI can bring you the antithesis in seconds, from anywhere. Only you can make the synthesis.

So how can we shape a more humane future with a technology that could threaten our humanity? My answer is abstention. Knowing what to hold back, at every step, so that AI empowers people rather than replaces them. Not a plain no, nor a plain yes. A "but" you can explain, and stand by.
